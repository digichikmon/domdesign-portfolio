Current Role · PMU Québec · Ongoing since 2022

Evacuation Plans — PMU Québec

Fire-prevention signage and evacuation plan drafting for buildings and factories across Québec — technical work I produce as part of my employment, not a single project with a start and end.

01

Overview & my role

Every evacuation plan is a piece of life-safety equipment before it's a piece of design — it has to work for someone reading it in smoke, low light, or a genuine emergency.

I work as an Evacuation Plan Technician / Graphic Designer at PMU Québec, producing the printed evacuation plans and fire-prevention signage that Québec's building and fire-prevention codes require for factories, retail spaces, offices, and institutional buildings. I take architectural floor plans from the project team and turn them into clear, code-compliant evacuation diagrams — routes, exits, assembly points, and the standardized symbol set for extinguishers, pull stations, and alarm equipment — ready for print and installation. This is ongoing production work, not a fixed engagement: new sites come in continuously, and existing plans get revised as buildings change.

  • Convert architectural floor plans into evacuation-route diagrams with correct exit paths and assembly points.
  • Apply a standardized fire-safety symbol legend consistently across every plan.
  • Lay out plans for legibility at the print sizes actually used on-site — not just on a screen.
  • Coordinate with fire-safety and project teams on code requirements and site-specific details.
  • Revise and reissue plans as buildings and occupancies change.

02

How a plan gets built

  1. Stage 1

    Site & Floor Plan Intake

    Input from the project team

    Architectural floor plans and site details come in from the project and fire-safety teams — the starting reference for every drawing.

  2. Stage 2

    Route & Symbol Drafting

    Core drafting work

    Exit routes, "You Are Here" points, and the standardized symbol legend (extinguishers, pull stations, alarms, assembly points) get placed and drawn against the floor plan.

  3. Stage 3

    Code Review & Print Layout

    Compliance & legibility pass

    The layout is checked against Québec's building and fire-prevention code requirements and formatted for legibility at real installed sizes, not just on-screen.

  4. Stage 4

    Delivery & Signage Production

    Ongoing

    Final files go out for printed signage installation on-site. Plans get revised and reissued whenever a building's layout or occupancy changes.

03

What the work requires

  • Legibility under stress — poor lighting, smoke, and a reader who does not have time to interpret a busy layout.
  • Durability and clarity across the different physical sizes plans are actually printed and mounted at.
  • Strict consistency in the fire-safety symbol legend, plan after plan.
  • Working accurately from architectural source drawings that are not always design-ready.
  • Treating every plan as a working safety document, not a decorative one.

The core discipline: a decorative instinct is the wrong instinct here. Every layout choice is judged against one question — will this be instantly readable by someone who has never seen this building before, under the worst conditions the building could be in? That constraint shapes everything, from typography to symbol placement to how much white space a page can actually afford.

05

What this role has taught me

  • Precision is not a stylistic choice in technical work — it's the whole job. A plan that's almost right is a plan that's wrong.
  • Production-floor thinking transfers directly: knowing how a file behaves at real print size, in real materials, before it leaves my screen.
  • Working inside strict code and legibility constraints has made my freelance branding and print work more disciplined, not less creative — clarity and hierarchy have to earn their place either way.
  • Ongoing, revision-based work rewards consistent systems over one-off cleverness — the symbol legend and layout grid have to hold up across hundreds of plans, not just one.
